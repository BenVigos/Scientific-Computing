\section{Introduction} \label{intro}

\IEEEPARstart{N}{umerical} methods provide a practical framework for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) that describe wave propagation, diffusion, and other transport phenomena. In this work, we study three representative problems: the one-dimensional wave equation, the two-dimensional diffusion equation, and iterative methods for solving the Laplace equation.

The first problem considers the one-dimensional wave equation

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial t^2}
=
c^2 \frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial x^2},
\end{equation}

which models vibrations of a stretched string of length $L=1$ with fixed boundary conditions $\Psi(0,t)=\Psi(1,t)=0$. The solution $\Psi(x,t)$ represents the displacement of the string. The equation is discretized using second-order central differences in both space and time, resulting in an explicit time-stepping scheme. This method is conditionally stable under the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition and is well suited for conservative oscillatory systems due to its second-order accuracy.

The second problem addresses mass transport governed by the two-dimensional diffusion equation

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}
=
D \nabla^2 c,
\label{eq:finite_difference}
\end{equation}

where $c(x,y,t)$ denotes the concentration and $D$ the diffusion coefficient. The domain is the unit square $0 \le x,y \le 1$, with fixed concentrations at the top and bottom boundaries and periodic boundary conditions in the $x$-direction. Using a standard five-point stencil, the equation is discretized with an explicit forward-time central-space scheme. Stability imposes the restriction
\[
\frac{4D \Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \le 1.
\]

When only the steady-state solution is of interest, the time-dependent problem reduces to the Laplace equation

\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 c = 0.
\end{equation}

Discretization yields a linear system where each grid value equals the average of its four neighbors. Rather than solving this system directly, iterative methods are employed. The Jacobi method updates all grid points using values from the previous iteration, while the Gauss–Seidel method uses newly computed values immediately, improving convergence. Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR) further accelerates convergence by introducing a relaxation parameter $\omega$. Iterations are terminated when the maximum update between successive iterations falls below a prescribed tolerance.

Together, these problems illustrate fundamental techniques in scientific computing: explicit time integration of hyperbolic and parabolic PDEs, stability constraints for finite difference schemes, and iterative solution of large sparse linear systems.

\section{Methodology}

    \subsection{Vibrating String}
    
        The one–dimensional wave equation
        
        is discretized using a second–order accurate central finite difference scheme in both space and time.
        
        The second derivative is obtained by summing the Taylor expansions of $f(x+h)$ and $f(x-h)$ about $x$, yielding
        
        \begin{align}
        f''(x)=\frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}
        +\mathcal{O}(h^2).
        \label{eq:2nd_der}
        \end{align}
        
        Applying this approximation to both temporal and spatial derivatives gives the explicit update scheme
        
        \begin{equation}
        \psi_j^{n+1}
        =
        2\psi_j^n
        -
        \psi_j^{n-1}
        +
        \left(\frac{c\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)^2
        \left(
        \psi_{j+1}^n
        -
        2\psi_j^n
        +
        \psi_{j-1}^n
        \right),
        \label{eq:wave_scheme}
        \end{equation}
        
        The scheme may equivalently be written in matrix form using the discrete Laplacian operator. For fixed Dirichlet boundaries, $g_0=g_N=0$, the boundary contributions vanish.
        
        The method is explicit and conditionally stable. Stability requires that the Courant number
        
        \begin{equation}
        \lambda
        =
        \frac{c\Delta t}{\Delta x}
        \end{equation}
        
        satisfies the CFL condition
        
        \begin{equation}
        \lambda \le 1.
        \label{eq:wave_stability}
        \end{equation}
        
        For all simulations, the wave speed is set to $c=1$ and the time step to $\Delta t = 0.001$. The spatial grid spacing is chosen such that the CFL condition is satisfied.



    



\subsection{Time Dependent Diffusion Equation}

To investigate the two-dimensional time-dependent diffusion equation, a numerical simulation was developed using an explicit finite difference scheme. The numerical results were subsequently validated against an exact analytical solution to ensure the fidelity of the computational model.


The governing time-dependent diffusion equation is given by equation \ref{eq:finite_difference}

The spatial domain was discretized into $N = 50$ intervals along both axes, yielding a uniform grid spacing of $\delta x = \delta y = 1/N$. Time was discretized into steps of $\delta t$. Applying a standard explicit finite difference approximation to Equation \ref{eq:finite_difference} yields the numerical update scheme as in equation:

    \begin{equation}
        c_{i,j}^{k+1}=c_{i,j}^{k}+\frac{\delta t D}{\delta x^{2}}(c_{i+1,j}^{k}+c_{i-1,j}^{k}+c_{i,j+1}^{k}+c_{i,j-1}^{k}-4c_{i,j}^{k})
    \end{equation}

To guarantee numerical stability for this explicit five-point stencil, the time step $\delta t$ must strictly satisfy the von Neumann stability condition:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{4\delta t D}{\delta x^{2}} \le 1
    \label{eq:stability}
\end{equation}
In the computational implementation, $\delta t$ was dynamically calculated using a safety factor of $0.2$ to ensure robust adherence to this theoretical limit. The simulation was initialized with a zero-concentration state across the domain, $c(x,y; t=0) = 0$. The boundary conditions dictated by the physical system were rigorously enforced at each time step:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dirichlet Boundaries (y-axis):} Fixed concentrations were maintained at the top and bottom of the domain, such that $c(x,y=1;t)=1$ and $c(x,y=0;t)=0$ respectively. Computationally, the first and last rows of the concentration matrix were explicitly overwritten with these fixed values after every iteration.
    \item \textbf{Periodic Boundaries (x-axis):} The domain exhibits periodicity in the $x$-direction, formulated as $c(x=0,y;t) = c(x=1,y;t)$. To implement this without requiring ghost nodes, modulo arithmetic was utilized to map the left neighbor of the $i=0$ boundary to $i=N-1$, and the right neighbor of $i=N$ to $i=1$. This seamlessly wraps the grid array, simulating an infinite lateral expanse.
\end{itemize}

Every 100 iterations, the numerical array was sampled, and the 1D concentration profile along the $y$-axis was appended to a CSV file. This lightweight tracking allowed for efficient post-processing and graphical rendering. To verify the accuracy of the numerical solver, the simulated profiles were compared against the exact analytical solution. Because the boundary conditions and initial state are uniform across $x$, the true solution is invariant with respect to the $x$-coordinate, reducing the problem to a 1D functional dependence on $y$ and $t$.

The analytical solution is expressed as an infinite series of complementary error functions:
\begin{equation}
    c(y,t) = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left[ \text{erfc}\left(\frac{1-y+2i}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) - \text{erfc}\left(\frac{1+y+2i}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) \right]
    \label{eq:analytical}
\end{equation}

The Python implementation computed this analytical solution truncated to the first 20 terms, as the series converges extremely rapidly. The numerical solution data was successfully captured and plotted against the analytical curves at discrete time intervals $t \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$. The convergence of the discrete scatter points onto the continuous analytical curves served as primary validation of the explicit finite difference methodology.



\subsection{Iterative Schemes}
The Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel and SOR schemes (see \ref{intro}) were implemented on a $N \times N$ grid with uniform spacing of $\delta x = \delta y = 1/N$.  Veritical and horizontal boundary conditions as described in section previously were used. The tolerance for a stopping condition  was set to $10^{-5}$ across all experiments, and the iteration count $k$ was defined as the number of full traversals of the interior grid points required to reach that tolerance. 

For validation, each scheme was compared against the analytical solution, and their respective MSEs were plotted point-wise. To further investigate the properties of each scheme, a convergence plot was also created. 

\subsubsection{Relaxation Parameter Dependence}
The optimal relaxation parameter $\omega_{opt}$ was defined as minimising the value of $k$ needed for SOR convergence for a given problem.To determine the $\omega_{opt}$ an initial sweep was performed over $\omega \in [0.5, 2.5)$ with step size $\Delta \omega =0.05$. Subsequently, a more refined sweep ($\Delta=0.01$) was performed around the vicinity of the $\omega$ resulting in the minimal iteration count. 


\subsubsection{Internal Objects in a Grid}
Internal objects were encoded using a boolean mask over a computational grid. Two types of objects were considered: sinks and insulators. For sinks, grid points belonging to the object mask were set to zero at each iteration. Insulators were implemented by enforcing a Neumann boundary condition \[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial n} = 0,
\]  at the grid points encoding edges of the insulator, thus preventing flux from moving across it. 

In order to investigate the presence of objects on the SOR scheme, iteration counts were measured as a function of placement and size and final concentration fields were stored for comparison.

    \subsection{Time dependent diffusion equation}
        The numerical simulation of the two-dimensional time-dependent diffusion equation was executed successfully using the explicit finite difference scheme. The temporal evolution of the concentration profile was recorded and analyzed to validate the computational model against established analytical frameworks. As defined by the problem's symmetry and boundary conditions, the concentration $c$ varies exclusively along the $y$-axis, rendering the profile uniform across any cross-section of the $x$-axis. The simulation data, extracted from the geometric center of the domain ($x=0.5$), demonstrates the progressive diffusion of the concentration from the upper boundary ($y=1$) towards the lower boundary ($y=0$). 

        Initially, the concentration is zero throughout the domain. As time progresses, a gradient forms. By plotting the numerical results at specific discrete times ($t = 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, \text{and } 1.0$), we observe the deepening penetration of the concentration into the domain. For $t\rightarrow\infty$ the concentration profile is simply a straight line. The simulation accurately captures this physical phenomenon, clearly showing that as $t$ reaches $1.0$ and beyond, the system approaches the steady-state linear limit, characterized mathematically by $\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}c(y,t)=y$.

\section{Discussion}

    \subsection{Vibrating string}
        The grid convergence study performed confirms the expected accuracy of $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$. The slightly higher than expected slope can be attributed to the points for which $\Delta x \approx c \Delta t $, which have a lower error than the theoretical one. This occurs because the leading second order truncation term vanishes due to cancellation between the spatial and temporal descritization errors. It was observed than when the grid convergence study was performed for a time which is a multiple of the wave's period, the slope of the graph was close to 4, suggesting a complete cancellation of the second order truncation term when the solution is the same as the initial condition.

        The results of the grid convergence study mean we can be quite confident about the results observed in \cref{fig:wave_heat}. The behaviour observed for the two first initial conditions is exactly what one would expect for a wave on a string with two closed ends with a number of antinodes equal to the wave number and a number of nodes one more than that of the antinodes. The behaviour observed for the third initial condition is more difficult to into it. However it is  quite characteristic of travelling waves on a string. 

    \subsection{Time dependent wave equation}
        The findings of this simulation demonstrate the robust capability of the explicit finite difference method to model the two-dimensional time-dependent diffusion equation. The excellent agreement between the numerical results and the exact analytical solution provides strong validation for the computational framework.

        As observed in the temporal evolution of the concentration profile, the numerical model accurately captures the physical behavior of the diffusion process. The system transitions smoothly from an initial state of zero internal concentration to a steady-state linear gradient, satisfying the theoretical limit $\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}c(y,t)=y$. The exceptionally low absolute error magnitudes (on the order of $10^{-4}$) at finite times confirm that the spatial resolution ($\delta x = 1/50$) and the dynamically scaled time step $\delta t$ sufficiently minimized truncation errors. Crucially, adhering strictly to the stability criterion $\frac{4\delta tD}{\delta x^{2}}\le1$ prevented the explicit scheme from diverging.
        
        Furthermore, the 2D spatial analysis verified the correct implementation of the system's boundary conditions. The horizontal uniformity of the concentration field confirms that the periodic boundary conditions applied along the $x$-axis effectively simulated an infinite lateral expanse without introducing artificial edge artifacts. 

        
    \subsection{Iterative Schemes}
        While Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel, and SOR all converge to the same steady-state solution, their rates and errors differ significantly. 
        Internal objects modify convergence behaviour in a significant way. Sinks reduce the number of effective degrees of freedom by effectively enforcing Dirichlet values on the interior points. As a result, certain sink placements and sizes reduce the $k$ needed to reach convergence. Sink positions near regions of strong concentration gradient introduce more contrast and require additional time to converge. The non-monotonic dependence of iteration count on sink area can also be then explained by that relationship: increasing area reduces unknowns, but the placement can make the calculations more complicated.
        In contrast, insulating objects preserve unknowns but essentially alter the connectivity between grid points. By enforcing zero normal flux, insulators obstruct diffusion and partition or disturb the domain, which results in slower convergence.
        This demonstrates that the convergence of iterative schemes is determined not only by the grid size and solver parameters but also by the structure of and within the domain.
        
\section{Conclusion}
This report applied finite difference methods to three representative PDE problems: the one-dimensional wave equation, the two-dimensional time-dependent diffusion equation, and the steady-state Laplace equation. For the wave equation, the explicit second-order scheme produced stable solutions under the CFL condition and the grid study confirmed the expected $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$ accuracy. For diffusion, the explicit solution matched the analytical well at multiple times while satisfying the von Neumann stability constraint, and the solution approached the expected steady state $c(y)=y$ with correct periodic behaviour in $x$.
For the Laplace problem, Jacobi, Gauss--Seidel, and SOR converged to the same steady-state solution but with substantially different efficiency.  Introducing internal objects showed that convergence depends strongly on domain structure.
